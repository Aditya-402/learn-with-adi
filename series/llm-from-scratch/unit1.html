What is an LLM?

A large language model is a neural network trained to understand, generate and respond to human-like text. That is the whole definition. The interesting part is the word large, which is doing two jobs at once:

  • Large in parameters. Parameters are the adjustable weights inside the network. Modern models have tens or hundreds of billions of them.
  • Large in data. They are trained on enormous quantities of text — sometimes a sizeable fraction of the public internet.

And what are all those parameters tuned to do? One thing: predict the next word in a sequence. That's it. It sounds far too simple to produce anything impressive, and honestly, it surprised a lot of researchers that it works as well as it does.

Key idea

Next-word prediction is a good training task because language is already a sequence. To learn context, structure and relationships between words, the model doesn't need anyone to explain grammar to it — it just needs to keep guessing what comes next and keep being corrected.

One caution on vocabulary before we go further. When we say a model "understands" language, we mean it processes and produces text that is coherent and contextually appropriate. We do not mean it has human-like consciousness or comprehension. Keep that distinction sharp — it prevents a lot of confused thinking later.

What LLMs are actually used for

Because they parse and generate unstructured text well, LLMs turn up almost anywhere text is involved: machine translation, sentiment analysis, summarisation, writing fiction and articles, writing computer code, powering chatbots and virtual assistants, and retrieving knowledge from large specialised document collections in fields like medicine and law.

There's a pattern worth noticing here. Before LLMs, each of these was a separate model. You built one system for translation, another for classification, another for summarisation — each with its own architecture and its own labelled dataset.

The shift

Earlier NLP models were narrow specialists that were excellent at one task. An LLM is a generalist: one model, many tasks, no retraining. That change — from many narrow models to one broad one — is the real story of the last few years.

Traditional methods were genuinely good at categorisation and pattern matching that could be captured with handcrafted rules. Where they fell down was anything needing complex understanding and generation: following detailed instructions, doing contextual analysis, producing coherent original text. Writing an email from a list of keywords was out of reach for the previous generation. It's trivial now.

Why pretraining needs no labels

Here is the trick that makes the whole thing affordable. Traditional supervised learning needs labelled examples, and labels are expensive — a human has to make each one. Pretraining sidesteps this entirely using self-supervised learning: the model generates its own labels from the input data.

How? Take any sentence. The words up to position n are the input; the word at position n+1 is the label. Nobody had to annotate anything — the text labels itself. Try it below.

Why build your own?

Custom LLMs tailored to a domain can outperform general-purpose ones — BloombergGPT for finance is a known example. Beyond accuracy there are three practical reasons: privacy (your sensitive data never leaves your infrastructure), deployment (a smaller model can run directly on a laptop or phone, cutting latency and server cost), and autonomy (you control updates, not a vendor).

The transformer architecture

Nearly every modern LLM rests on the transformer, introduced in the 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need". It was originally built for machine translation — English to German and French — and it has two halves:

  • The encoder reads the input text and turns it into a series of vectors that capture its meaning in context.
  • The decoder takes those vectors and generates the output text, one word at a time.

Connecting them is the self-attention mechanism, which lets the model weigh how important every word is relative to every other word. That's what allows it to handle long-range dependencies — to connect a pronoun to a noun twelve words back. Unit 3 implements it properly; for now, just get a feel for it.

Two descendants: BERT and GPT

Later models kept one half of the transformer and threw away the other.

  • BERT (bidirectional encoder representations from transformers) keeps the encoder. It's trained on masked word prediction — hide a word, predict it — and because it can look in both directions, it's strong at classification. X (formerly Twitter) has used BERT to detect toxic content.
  • GPT (generative pretrained transformer) keeps the decoder. It's trained on next-word prediction, sees only what came before, and is built to generate.

Zero-shot and few-shot

A pretrained GPT can often do a task it was never fine-tuned for, just from how you phrase the input. Give it no examples and it's working zero-shot; give it a handful inside the prompt and it's working few-shot. No retraining, no architecture change — the examples just sit in the input.

Transformers vs LLMs — not the same word

The terms get used interchangeably, but they don't fully overlap. Not all transformers are LLMs — transformers are also used for computer vision. And not all LLMs are transformers — some are built on recurrent or convolutional architectures, usually to improve computational efficiency. Throughout this series, "LLM" means a transformer-based, GPT-like model.

Common confusion

LLM ≠ transformer ≠ GPT. They're nested, not interchangeable. A transformer is an architecture (some transformers aren't language models at all). An LLM is any large model of language (a few aren't transformers). A GPT is one specific family: a decoder-only transformer LLM trained on next-word prediction — the intersection this series builds. The data behind the model

The non-obvious bit

Wikipedia is only 3 billion tokens — under 1% of what was available — yet it makes up 3% of training, meaning it was read roughly three times over. CommonCrawl, the biggest source by far, was sampled at less than half of one pass. Quantity is not the same as weight. The engineers deliberately fed the model more of the good text.

Two more numbers worth carrying with you. The subsets total 499 billion tokens, but the model was trained on only 300 billion — the GPT-3 authors never explained why. And pretraining cost an estimated $4.6 million in cloud computing credits. That's why nobody pretrains from scratch casually, and why openly available pretrained weights matter so much.

What this means for us

We'll write real pretraining code and run it on a small dataset — every computation in this series runs on ordinary consumer hardware. Then we'll load openly available pretrained weights into the architecture we built, which lets us skip the expensive stage and go straight to fine-tuning.

A closer look at GPT

Compared with the full transformer, GPT's architecture is startlingly plain: it's just the decoder, with the encoder removed. It reads left to right, one direction only, and it was pretrained on nothing but next-word prediction.

Because each new word is chosen based on the sequence that precedes it — and then becomes part of the sequence for the next prediction — GPT is called an autoregressive model. That feedback loop is what keeps generated text coherent.

Scale is where the size went, not complexity. The original transformer stacked its blocks 6 times. GPT-3 has 96 transformer layers and 175 billion parameters.

Emergent behaviour

GPT models can translate between languages — despite being decoder-only models trained purely to predict the next word, with no translation objective anywhere in training. Nobody taught it; the ability emerged from exposure to vast multilingual text. This is why one model can replace many specialised ones, and it's the single most surprising result in the unit.
