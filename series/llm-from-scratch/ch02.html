Why text has to become numbers

Neural networks perform arithmetic — multiplications and additions on continuous values. Text is categorical: the word "cat" is not a number, and it isn't 0.7 more than "bat". So raw text simply cannot enter a network. We need to represent words as continuous-valued vectors.

Converting data into a vector format is called embedding. At its core an embedding is a mapping from discrete objects — words, images, whole documents — to points in a continuous vector space.

Different data types need different embedding models: one built for text will not work on audio or video. There are also embeddings for sentences, paragraphs and whole documents (popular in retrieval-augmented generation), but since we're building a GPT that generates text one word at a time, we focus on word embeddings.

What the vector space buys us

The pay-off is that position becomes meaning. One early and famous method, Word2Vec, trained a network to predict a word's context from the word, on the principle that words appearing in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. Plot the result and related words land near one another.

Key idea

We will not use Word2Vec. LLMs produce their own embeddings as part of the input layer, and those embeddings are updated during training — so they end up optimised for the specific task and data at hand, rather than borrowed from a general-purpose model. You'll build that layer later in this unit.

Splitting text into tokens

Before anything can be embedded, text must be broken into tokens — individual words and special characters, including punctuation. Our training text is "The Verdict", a public-domain short story by Edith Wharton, 20,479 characters long.

Keep the whitespace or drop it?

      
It depends on the application. Dropping it reduces memory and compute, which is why we drop it here. But keeping it matters for text where exact structure carries meaning — Python source code, where indentation is syntax, is the obvious case. The BPE tokenizer we reach further down keeps whitespace.

    

  


  
  

    

      
Turning tokens into token IDs

      
Tokens are still strings. To get integers we build a vocabulary: take every unique token in the training text, sort them alphabetically, and number them from zero. Applied to "The Verdict", that gives a vocabulary of 1,130 entries.

      
A tokenizer then needs two methods. encode splits text and maps each token to its ID. decode maps IDs back to tokens and rejoins them into readable text.

Special tokens

      
A crash on an unseen word is not acceptable, so we extend the vocabulary with two special entries:

      
    
        
  • <|unk|> — stands in for any word not in the vocabulary, instead of raising an error.
    • 
        
  • <|endoftext|> — inserted between unrelated documents. When you concatenate a thousand documents into one training stream, this marker tells the model that the text either side is unrelated.
    • 
      

      
Adding both takes the vocabulary from 1,130 to 1,132, with <|endoftext|> at ID 1130 and <|unk|> at 1131.

Other tokenizers use [BOS] (beginning of sequence), [EOS] (end of sequence) and [PAD] (padding, to equalise lengths within a batch). GPT's tokenizer uses none of them — only <|endoftext|>, which serves as both separator and padding.

    

  


  
  

    

      
Byte pair encoding

      
GPT models don't use <|unk|> at all. They use byte pair encoding, and it handles words it has never seen — perfectly, every time, with no special token.

      
The idea: start with a vocabulary of individual characters. Then repeatedly find the most frequent adjacent pair of symbols and merge it into a single new symbol. Do that a few thousand times and you end up with a vocabulary of characters, common subwords, and whole frequent words. An unknown word is simply broken into the largest pieces the vocabulary does contain — falling back to single characters if it must.

      
From here on we use OpenAI’s tiktoken library and calls a full implementation out of scope. It's worth seeing the algorithm run, though, so the lab below actually performs it.

Why this solves the unknown-word problem

      
Because the vocabulary always contains every single character, any string can be represented — worst case, character by character. There is no input BPE can fail on. That's why GPT needs no <|unk|> token, and why it can read a word like someunknownPlace and reproduce it exactly.

    


    

      
GPT-2’s tokenizer on real text

      
"Hello, do you like tea? <|endoftext|> In the sunlit terraces of someunknownPlace." encodes to:

 262, 4252, 18250, 8812, 2114, 286, 617, 34680, 27271, 13]

      
Note 50256 — that's <|endoftext|>, the highest ID in a 50,257-token vocabulary. And someunknownPlace, a word that exists nowhere, became the four tokens 617, 34680, 27271 plus punctuation — subwords, not an error.

Sampling with a sliding window

      
We now have a stream of token IDs. Training needs input–target pairs, and from Unit 1 you already know the rule: the target is the input shifted by one position.

      
Two settings control how those pairs are cut from the stream. Context size (called max_length in the code) is how many tokens go into one input. Stride is how far the window jumps before taking the next sample. Change them below and watch the batches change.

Teal marks tokens that also appeared in the previous sample. Set stride equal to context size and the teal disappears — every token is used exactly once, with no overlap between batches.

    


    

      
Why stride matters

      
A small stride squeezes more training samples out of the same text, but consecutive samples overlap heavily — and too much overlap increases overfitting. Setting stride equal to context size uses the dataset fully without repeating a single token. That's the setting the real data loader uses.

Token embeddings

      
Final step of the conversion: token IDs become embedding vectors. An embedding layer holds a weight matrix with one row per vocabulary entry and one column per embedding dimension. The weights start as small random values and are optimised during training, exactly like any other network weights.

      
Despite the name, the operation is not a multiplication — it's a lookup. Token ID 3 retrieves row 3. That's all.

For those who know one-hot encoding

      
An embedding lookup is mathematically identical to one-hot encoding a token ID and multiplying by a weight matrix — it just skips the wasteful multiplication by a vector of zeros. That equivalence is why the embedding layer is a genuine neural network layer and can be trained by backpropagation like everything else.

    

  


  
  

    

      
Encoding word positions

      
There's one flaw left. The embedding layer maps a token ID to the same vector regardless of where it sits in the sequence. And as Unit 3 will show, the self-attention mechanism is itself position-agnostic. So as things stand, "the dog bit the man" and "the man bit the dog" would look disturbingly similar to the model.

      
The fix is to add a second embedding — one indexed by position rather than by token — and simply add the two vectors together.

Positional values here are illustrative. In PyTorch they come from a second nn.Embedding(context_length, output_dim), initialised randomly and — in GPT — learned during training rather than fixed by a formula as in the original transformer.

    


    

      
Absolute vs relative

      
    
        
  • Absolute positional embeddings attach a unique vector to each specific position: first slot, second slot, and so on. This is what OpenAI's GPT models use, and what we implement.
    • 
        
  • Relative positional embeddings encode how far apart two tokens are rather than where each one sits. The advantage is better generalisation to sequence lengths never seen in training.
    • 
      

      
At full scale the shapes work out like this: a batch of 8 samples × 4 tokens becomes an 8 × 4 × 256 token-embedding tensor. The positional tensor is 4 × 256 — one vector per slot — and PyTorch broadcasts it across all eight samples in the batch. Add them and you get 8 × 4 × 256 input embeddings, ready for the transformer.

    


    

      
The unit in one line

      
text → tokens → token IDs → token embeddings → + positional embeddings → input embeddings. That last tensor is what Unit 3 feeds into the attention mechanism. Everything in this unit exists to produce it.
