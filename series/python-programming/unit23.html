23.1

The first picture — and the three lines that make it honest

Read · ~3 minPractice · ~10 min

The problem. Unit 22 ended with a column of numbers: nine marks, three subjects, a mean each. You read them, and you probably didn't notice the thing that matters. Humans are bad at scanning columns and excellent at spotting a shape. Twenty numbers in a list is homework; twenty numbers as a line is a story you take in at a glance. How do you turn one into the other?

The idea, in plain words. matplotlib is a drawing pad, and pyplot is the set of pens. You import it once — everyone in the world writes it the same way, import matplotlib.pyplot as plt — and then each plt. line adds one thing to the pad that is currently open. plt.plot(x, y) draws the line. And then, before you stop:

  • plt.title("...") — what am I looking at?
  • plt.xlabel("...") — what does sideways mean?
  • plt.ylabel("...") — what does up mean?

Three lines. They take four seconds to type and they are the entire difference between a chart and a scribble, because an unlabelled chart is a lie waiting to happen — not because it says anything false, but because a reader cannot check what they cannot read. A rising line with no axis names could be sales, could be temperature, could be your heart rate looking at the sales. Make it a habit now, while your charts are small and nobody is depending on them; the habit is what survives into the ones that matter.

Trace it by hand — what each line adds to the pad
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt days = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] pages = [12, 20, 17, 26, 31] plt.plot(days, pages) plt.title("Pages read this week") plt.xlabel("Day") plt.ylabel("Pages")

Line 1: the pens arrive. Nothing is drawn yet — this is the one line that makes the rest possible, and it is heavier than a normal import (the page fetches the library on first run, so give it a few seconds).

Lines 2–3: two ordinary lists from Unit 3's world. matplotlib is not fussy: it takes lists, it takes NumPy arrays, it takes pandas columns.

Line 4: a blank pad appears and gets one line drawn on it, pairing them up — (1, 12), (2, 20), (3, 17), (4, 26), (5, 31). Note the pairing rule: the two lists must be the same length, and the nth of one goes with the nth of the other.

Lines 5–7: three captions written onto that same pad. The pad stays open until the program ends, and then this page hands you the picture.

There is no print here and no output text — the result of this program is the image itself, drawn below the (empty) output box.

Fig. 1 — Identical data, identical drawing code. The right-hand pad had title, xlabel and ylabel added; that is the whole difference. Adopt it as a rule: no chart leaves your screen unlabelled.
Common confusion

Tutorials elsewhere end every example with plt.show(). In a normal Python program that line is what pops the picture window open — but in this page (and in Colab) the figure is collected and displayed for you, so plt.show() is unnecessary here and will simply do nothing. Leave it out in these notes; add it back the day you run a .py file from a terminal.

23.1 · P2The chart that lies

This one is labelled, which is worse than unlabelled — it is labelled wrongly. The lists say rainfall per month, but the captions claim something else entirely, and the axes are swapped. Run it, read the mismatch, and fix all three caption lines so the chart tells the truth.

Show solution
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

month = [6, 7, 8, 9, 10]

The first argument to plot goes sideways, the second goes up — so the first list's meaning belongs in xlabel and the second's in ylabel. Swapped labels are the commonest chart bug in the wild, and no error message will ever warn you.

23.1 · P3Know the number before you draw it

A chart shows you a shape; it rarely gives you an exact figure, so a good study prints the headline numbers alongside the picture. Using these two lists, print the total sales and the name of the best month. (max, sum and .index() from Unit 6 are all you need — no plotting in this one.)

Total: 98 Best month: May
Show solution
months = ["Jan", "Feb", "Mar", "Apr", "May"]

23.2

Four charts, and when to use which

Read · ~4 minPractice · ~10 min

The problem. You have a line chart, so everything starts looking like a line chart — including things that have no business being one. Sales for four cities drawn as a line implies Chennai flows into Pune, which is nonsense. Choosing the wrong chart doesn't produce an error; it produces a confident, well-labelled, wrong impression. So: which picture for which question?

The idea, in plain words. There are dozens of chart types and you need four. Each one answers a different shape of question, and you pick by asking what your question is, not by what looks nice.

your questionchartthe call
How did one thing change over time?lineplt.plot(x, y)
Which category is biggest?barplt.bar(names, values)
Do these two numbers move together?scatterplt.scatter(x, y)
What does one column's spread look like?histogramplt.hist(values, bins=…)

Three of those take two lists, as you'd expect. The histogram is the odd one and the interesting one: it takes a single list of numbers and counts them into ranges called bins — how many marks between 50 and 60, how many between 60 and 70 — then draws one bar per bin. You do not tell it the heights; it counts them for you. That is why a histogram answers a question no other chart here can: not "what is each value" but "what is the shape of all of them together" — clumped in the middle, spread wide, two humps, one long tail.

Fig. 2 — Bar and histogram look like cousins and are not: a bar chart's bars are categories you chose and can be reordered freely, while a histogram's bars are ranges of one number, touching, in the only order they can be. That is why histogram bars are drawn with no gaps.
Key idea
Pick the chart from the question, not from the data. Time on the bottom → line. Names on the bottom → bar. Two measurements per thing → scatter. One measurement, many things, and you want its shape → histogram.
Worked example

Task: the same small dataset, asked four different ways. Thinking: plt.figure() opens a fresh pad, so one program can draw several pictures — you'll get four images stacked under the output, in order. Read each title and check it against the shape underneath.

23.2 · P4Stretch — histogram, and what bins does

Twenty marks, one list. Draw a labelled histogram with bins=4, look at it, then change to bins=10 and run again. Same numbers, two different stories — decide for yourself which one you'd put in a report, and why. Run-only, deliberately: this is a judgement, not a check.

Show solution
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

marks = [38, 44, 51, 53, 55, 58, 59, 61, 62, 63,
         64, 66, 68, 69, 71, 74, 77, 81, 85, 94]

plt.hist(marks, bins=10)
plt.title("Spread of marks, 20 students")
plt.xlabel("Mark")
plt.ylabel("Number of students")

Few bins: a smooth hump, and detail is lost. Many bins: every wobble shows, including wobbles that are just twenty students being twenty students. bins is an honest judgement call — which is why a histogram should always say how many it used, or at least be shown next to the numbers.

23.3

Making it readable — size, colour and a legend

Read · ~3 minPractice · ~9 min

The problem. Two cities, one chart. You call plt.plot twice, both lines land on the same pad — good — and now nobody, including you tomorrow, can tell which line is which. Meanwhile the default figure is a small square that squashes twelve month names into an unreadable smear.

The idea, in plain words. Three knobs, and you will use all three constantly:

  • Size. plt.figure(figsize=(8, 4)) — width and height in inches, set before you draw. Wide and short suits time-lines; tall suits horizontal bars with long names.
  • Colour and marker. plt.plot(x, y, color="#5b4b9e", marker="o"). Colour names ("teal", "crimson") work too, and marker puts a shape on each data point so a reader can see where the real measurements are, as opposed to the line that merely joins them.
  • A legend. Give each series a label="Chennai" when you draw it, then call plt.legend() once, at the end. The legend is a box matching each label to its line — and it appears only if you ask; labels alone draw nothing.

One honest note on colour, and it matters more than it sounds. Roughly one person in twelve sees red and green as much the same, and plenty of charts get printed in black and white or screenshotted through a filter. So never let colour be the only difference: pair it with a marker shape or a dash style (linestyle="--"), and where the chart is simple enough, label the line directly on the picture rather than making the reader glance back and forth to a legend at all. Colour should be the pleasant reinforcement of a distinction, not the distinction itself.

Trace it by hand — two lines on one pad
plt.figure(figsize=(7, 4)) plt.plot(months, chennai, color="#5b4b9e", marker="o", label="Chennai") plt.plot(months, pune, color="#2b7a78", marker="s", label="Pune") plt.legend()

Line 1: a new pad, seven inches by four. Anything drawn from now on lands here.

Line 2: the first line is drawn, purple, with a round marker on each month. The label is written on a sticky note and filed — nothing about it is visible yet.

Line 3: a second line on the same pad (no new plt.figure(), so no new pad), teal, square markers, second sticky note filed.

Line 4: matplotlib collects the sticky notes and draws the legend box, choosing a corner that covers the least data. Remove this line and the labels stay filed away forever — the commonest cause of "my legend isn't showing".

Common confusion

plt.figure() starts a new picture; it does not clear the current one. So two plot calls with a figure() between them give you two charts with one line each, while two plot calls in a row give you one chart with two lines. If your second series has vanished, count your plt.figure() calls — and if you meant to compare, you want fewer of them, not more.

Worked example

Task: five months, two cities, one readable chart. Thinking: size first, then one plot per city carrying its own colour, marker and label, then captions, then the legend last.

Two products over six months. The drawing is done; the chart is unreadable. Make it readable: give the figure a sensible size, give each line a colour and a distinct marker, label both, add the three captions and the legend. Run-only — the test is whether a stranger could read it.

23.3 · P2The number behind the crossing

A chart shows you that two series diverge; it rarely tells you by exactly how much. Two teams, four quarters. Print the quarter with the biggest gap between them, and the size of that gap, in the exact shape shown. (abs() gives a difference without a minus sign; loop over the four positions.) No plotting here — this is the number you would print under the picture.

Biggest gap: Q3 (6)
Show solution
quarters = ["Q1", "Q2", "Q3", "Q4"]
team_a = [10, 14, 9, 18]
team_b = [12, 11, 15, 13]

best_q = ""
best_gap = 0
for i in range(len(quarters)):
    gap = abs(team_a[i] - team_b[i])
    if gap > best_gap:
        best_gap = gap
        best_q = quarters[i]

print(f"Biggest gap: {best_q} ({best_gap})")

Gaps of 2, 3, 6, 5 — Q3 wins. Pair this line with the two-series chart and your reader gets both the shape and the fact. A picture proposes; a number confirms.

23.4

Plotting straight from pandas — the short path

Read · ~3 minPractice · ~9 min

The problem. Your data is almost never two hand-typed lists. It is a DataFrame from Unit 22, possibly the result of a groupby, and pulling columns out into lists just to hand them to plt.bar is busywork — busywork that also throws away the column names you'd want as labels.

The idea, in plain words. pandas already knows about matplotlib. Every DataFrame and every Series has a .plot() method that draws itself:

df.plot(kind="bar", x="city", y="sales")

kind takes exactly the words you'd hope: "line", "bar", "barh", "scatter", "hist". And a groupby result is even shorter, because a Series already knows its own index — by_city.plot(kind="bar") uses the group names as the bottom axis without being told. One line from table to picture.

Two things to keep straight. First, .plot() is still matplotlib underneath, so you must import matplotlib.pyplot as plt as well, and you finish with the same plt.title / plt.xlabel / plt.ylabel — pandas guesses at labels, and its guesses are for you, not for a reader. Second, pandas adds a legend automatically whenever it plots a named column; with a single series that legend is noise, and legend=False removes it.

Trace it by hand — table to picture
sales = pd.DataFrame({"city": ["Chennai", "Pune", "Delhi"], "sales": [120, 95, 143]}) sales.plot(kind="bar", x="city", y="sales", legend=False) plt.title("Sales by city")

Line 1–2: the DataFrame from Unit 22 — three rows, two columns, and pandas remembers both column names. That memory is the whole advantage here.

Line 3: pandas opens a pad, reads x="city" for the categories and y="sales" for the heights, draws three bars, writes the city names underneath by itself, and suppresses the legend we don't need.

Line 4: an ordinary plt. call, landing on the pad pandas just opened — because it is the same matplotlib all along. Everything you learned in §23.1 and §23.3 still applies.

Worked example

Task: a four-row table, straight to a labelled bar chart. Thinking: build the DataFrame, one .plot() call with x and y named, then the three captions as usual. Compare the line count with doing it by hand — this is the shortest honest path there is.

23.4 · P1One table, two kinds

The rainfall table below. Draw it with df.plot(kind="line", x="month", y="rain_mm") and label it; then change kind to "bar" and run again. Both are defensible here — months are a time sequence and a set of categories — so decide which you prefer and be able to say why. Run-only.

Show solution
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rain = pd.DataFrame({
    "month": ["Jun", "Jul", "Aug", "Sep", "Oct"],
    "rain_mm": [45, 120, 180, 160, 210],
})

rain.plot(kind="bar", x="month", y="rain_mm", color="#2b7a78", legend=False)

The line emphasises the rise-and-fall; the bars emphasise "October was the wettest". Same data, different sentence. That choice is yours and you should make it on purpose.

23.4 · P2Plot a groupby result

Six rows, three cities, two days each — exactly Unit 22's shape. Group by city, take the mean of sales, and plot the result. The Series already carries the city names as its index, so .plot(kind="bar") needs no x or y at all. Label it properly and run.

Show solution
by_city = df.groupby("city")["sales"].mean()
print(by_city)

by_city.plot(kind="bar", color="#5b4b9e")
plt.title("Average daily sales by city")
plt.xlabel("City")
plt.ylabel("Mean sales (thousands)")

Group, aggregate, plot — three ideas, three lines, and the group names travel from the table to the axis without you touching them. This is the motion you will repeat for years.

23.4 · P3Print what the picture claims

Same six rows. Never publish a bar chart without being able to state its numbers. Compute the mean sales per city and print one line per city, one decimal place, in the exact shape below. (A grouped Series can be walked with for name, value in series.items(): — and note the order pandas gives you.)

Chennai: 130.0 Delhi: 150.0 Pune: 100.0
Show solution
by_city = df.groupby("city")["sales"].mean()
for name, value in by_city.items():
    print(f"{name}: {value:.1f}")

Alphabetical, because groupby sorts its keys — Chennai, Delhi, Pune, not the order they appeared in the table. Worth knowing before you claim your bar chart is "in order of sales".

23.5

seaborn, in the notebook

Read · ~4 minPractice · in Colab

The problem. Some perfectly ordinary questions turn matplotlib into a chore. "Average mark per subject, with a little bar showing how uncertain that average is" needs a groupby, a mean, a standard error and a call to plt.errorbar. "Marks by subject, coloured by student, side by side" needs a loop, manual bar offsets and a hand-built legend. You can write all that. You will not enjoy it twice.

The idea, in plain words. seaborn is a thin, well-mannered layer that sits on top of matplotlib and speaks DataFrame. You hand it the whole table and the names of the columns to use — data=df, x="subject", y="marks" — and it does the grouping, the aggregating, the colouring and the legend for you. Underneath it is still matplotlib, so every plt.title and plt.xlabel you know still works on a seaborn chart.

Two more gifts. First, its defaults are simply better-looking than raw matplotlib's — softer palette, grid lines, no fiddling. Second, and more useful: hue=. Name a third column as hue and seaborn splits every mark by that column, colours each group and builds the legend, in one word. Doing that by hand is the loop-and-offset misery above.

Read-only from here to the end of this section
seaborn cannot run in this page — it isn't one of the libraries the in-page Python can fetch, and that will not change. So the three listings below are for reading, not for running. Every one of them works as written in the notebook: ▶ open Unit 23 in Colab, where seaborn is already installed, and type them out there. This is the honest state of things, not a limitation worth hiding.
Listing 1 · sns.barplot — a category average, straight from the table
import seaborn as sns
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# marks is the nine-row DataFrame from 23.6: name, subject, marks
sns.barplot(data=marks, x="subject", y="marks")
plt.title("Average mark by subject")
plt.xlabel("Subject")
plt.ylabel("Mark")

What it does for you. No groupby, no .mean(). seaborn sees three subjects, averages the marks within each by itself, and draws one bar per subject — plus a thin vertical line on each bar showing how much the marks inside that group varied. That last part is the point: a bare average hides whether the group was tight or all over the place.

When it beats raw matplotlib. Whenever the bar heights are a summary of many rows rather than numbers you already have. In matplotlib you must compute the summary first; here the raw table is the input.

Listing 2 · sns.histplot — the shape of one column
import seaborn as sns
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

sns.histplot(data=marks, x="marks", bins=6, kde=True)
plt.title("Spread of all marks")
plt.xlabel("Mark")
plt.ylabel("Number of results")

What it does for you. The same histogram as plt.hist, named by column instead of extracted by hand — and kde=True lays a smooth curve over the bars, the "if we had thousands of results instead of nine, this is roughly the shape it would settle into" line. It is a guess, drawn honestly as a guess, and it makes a lumpy small-sample histogram much easier to read.

When it beats raw matplotlib. When you want the shape and the smoothed version, or when the numbers live in a DataFrame column you'd rather name than slice out.

Listing 3 · sns.scatterplot with hue — the third dimension
import seaborn as sns
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# study is a table with columns: hours, mark, subject
sns.scatterplot(data=study, x="hours", y="mark", hue="subject")
plt.title("Hours against marks, split by subject")
plt.xlabel("Hours studied per week")
plt.ylabel("Mark")

What it does for you. One word, hue="subject", and every dot is coloured by its subject with a matching legend drawn automatically. Now you can see not just "more hours, better marks" but whether that relationship is steeper for one subject than another — a question the plain scatter cannot even ask.

When it beats raw matplotlib. The moment a third column enters the picture. This is the single most common reason working analysts reach for seaborn — and, fair warning, the colours here are again doing the distinguishing, so for anything that matters add style="subject" too and let the marker shapes carry the same information.

Key idea
matplotlib draws what you tell it; seaborn works out what to draw from a table and a few column names. Learn matplotlib first — every seaborn chart is a matplotlib chart underneath, and when seaborn's answer isn't quite right, you fix it with the plt. calls you already know.
23.6

Your build — a mini data study

Read · ~3 minPractice · ~12 min

Three units, one motion. A study — the real thing, at small scale — is always these four beats: load the data, compute the numbers, draw the picture, write what you found. Skip the last beat and you have a slide deck; skip the third and nobody reads you; skip the second and you have a decoration.

Our data is the small student table: three students, three subjects, nine marks. We rebuild it in the program rather than reading a file, so every problem here stands alone — but everything below would be identical if the DataFrame had come from pd.read_csv, which is the only line that would change in real life.

Here is what the finished write-up sounds like, from the numbers the worked example prints. The findings: English is the weakest subject at an average of 74.0, six marks below Science's 80.0, and it is weak across all three students rather than being dragged down by one. Asha and Meera finish in a near dead heat — 238 and 237 total marks — but by very different routes: Meera tops Maths with 90 and comes last in English with 66, while Asha never drops below 72. Ravi trails on total at 221 yet posts the single best English mark of the group at 84, which suggests the English problem is a teaching or marking question rather than a Ravi question.

Three sentences. Each one names a number, and each one you could only have found by looking at the picture — but could only state by having the numbers. That is why the beats come in that order and why neither half is optional.

Trace it by hand — the four beats
marks = pd.DataFrame({...}) # 1. load by_subject = marks.groupby("subject")["marks"].mean() # 2. compute by_subject.plot(kind="bar") # 3. draw # "English is 6 marks below Science" # 4. write

Beat 1 gives you nine rows and three columns. In a real study this is pd.read_csv("marks.csv") and it is the only line that knows where the data lives.

Beat 2 collapses nine rows into three numbers: English 74.0, Maths 78.0, Science 80.0. These are the facts you will quote.

Beat 3 turns those three numbers into a shape your eye reads in a quarter of a second — and, crucially, might make you notice something you weren't looking for.

Beat 4 is the only beat with no library. It is also the only one anybody else will read.

Worked example

Task: the whole study, top to bottom, in one program. Thinking: load the table, print it so we can see what we have, group and print the subject means, then draw the labelled bar chart of exactly those means. Read the printed numbers first, then look at the picture and check that it says the same thing — a picture that disagrees with your numbers means a bug in one of them.

23.6 · P1Beat 1 — load, and check what you loaded

Rebuild the table and print two sanity checks before anything else: how many rows you got, and the overall mean mark to two decimals. Every study starts here, because the fastest way to publish nonsense is to skip the moment where you look at what you actually have.

Rows: 9 Mean mark: 77.33
Show solution
print("Rows:", len(marks))
print(f"Mean mark: {marks['marks'].mean():.2f}")

len(df) counts rows; the mean of all nine is 696 ÷ 9 = 77.333…, dressed to two places by :.2f. Note the single quotes inside the f-string's double quotes — Unit 22's habit, still paying off.

23.6 · P2Beat 2 — the three numbers you will quote

Group by subject, take the mean of the marks, and print one line per subject to one decimal place in the exact shape below. These are the numbers the findings paragraph quotes — and the ones the next problem's chart has to agree with.

English: 74.0 Maths: 78.0 Science: 80.0
Show solution
by_subject = marks.groupby("subject")["marks"].mean()
for subject, avg in by_subject.items():
    print(f"{subject}: {avg:.1f}")

Alphabetical order again, and the six-mark English-to-Science gap is now a fact rather than an impression. Printing a grouped Series with a loop instead of print(series) also means you control the formatting — worth it the moment anyone else has to read it.

B2Histogram, with its numbers

Ten test scores. Draw a labelled histogram with bins=5 and print the mean and median to one decimal place, in the exact shape below — the picture shows the shape, the two numbers pin it down. B4Stretch — group it, print it, draw it
